# Close CallBridge's human-voice gap without pretending

CallBridge sounds less human than the agents the user has heard mainly because of **timing**, not voice timbre. Its architecture is deliberately turn-based and half-duplex: semantic_vad runs at `eagerness: auto`, the server speech gate decides when to answer, and only then does gpt-realtime-2.1 begin to speak. That puts most reply gaps in the range listeners read as hesitation or trouble (roughly 700 ms and up). The agents that impressed the user were most likely built on the **full-duplex models released in September and October 2026**: OpenAI GPT-Live-1, xAI Grok Voice 3.0, and possibly Google Gemini 3.8 Live. These models listen while they speak, backchannel, and handle interruptions natively, and OpenAI itself says GPT-Live-1 beats gpt-realtime-2.1 by about 30 points on Full-Duplex-Bench. Others may have been premium-TTS pipelines (ElevenLabs, Cartesia), or used tricks CallBridge must not copy, such as fake office ambience, scripted "ums", or no AI disclosure. Using the user's own voice is technically feasible today, either through self-serve instant cloning in a hybrid TTS pipeline (ElevenLabs, Cartesia, Inworld) or through Grok Voice 3.0's Custom Voices API. However, it adds the least naturalness per unit of risk: 8 kHz phone audio strips much of what makes a voice recognizable, cross-lingual clones carry over the speaker's accent, and a familiar voice is exactly what makes friends and family forget the AI disclosure. The recommendation runs in three phases: (1) tune the current OpenAI stack to cut turn gaps and change the conversational register; (2) run a blind A/B bake-off behind the existing `VoiceAgent` interface of tuned gpt-realtime-2.1, Eleven Realtime, GPT-Live-1, Grok Voice 3.0 and a hybrid gpt-realtime-text-to-premium-TTS pipeline, across all five languages over real phone lines; (3) offer opt-in self-cloning only afterwards, with live consent capture, voice-specific disclosure and blocks on calls to voice-authenticating institutions.

> **How to read this report.** It synthesizes six research notes compiled on 22 October 2026. In that environment, direct page fetches to almost every primary domain (OpenAI, Google, ElevenLabs, Artificial Analysis, FCC and others) failed. **Most figures therefore come from search-result summaries of the cited pages**, and many sources are vendor blogs or third-party aggregators. Prices, latencies, benchmark scores, release dates and legal status are all moving quickly and **should be verified on the primary pages before any decision commits money or code**. Nothing specific to gpt-realtime-2.1 beyond its pricing page and OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 comparison was found. Behavior described for "gpt-realtime-2" (preambles, reasoning effort) is assumed to carry over to 2.1. The legal sections are not legal advice.

## Rival agents win on timing and full duplex, not timbre

Human conversation runs on very tight timing. Stivers et al.'s ten-language study found that every language minimizes silence between turns, varying only within roughly ±250 ms of a common mean ([emcawiki](https://emcawiki.net/Stivers-etal2009)). Later reviews put the modal gap at about **200 ms** ([Magyari review](https://uis.brage.unit.no/uis-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/3136187/magyari_predictions_in_conversation_AMMf45188.pdf?sequence=2)). Long gaps also carry meaning. Kendrick and Torreira found that only at transitions of **700 ms or more** do refusals and disagreements clearly outnumber agreeable replies ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4689543)). In simulated phone calls, Roberts et al. found that listeners perceived less willingness and weaker agreement as silence grew from 0 to 600 to 1,200 ms ([emcawiki](https://emcawiki.net/Roberts2006)). An agent that pauses for a second before every answer therefore sounds hesitant and machine-like even when its words and voice are perfect. Practitioners treat roughly **800 ms voice-to-voice** as the practical ceiling ([Twig](https://www.twig.so/blog/voice-ai-agents-latency-budget-800ms)), and say production agents typically land between 800 ms and 2 s ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/glossary/latency)).

CallBridge's design pushes it toward the slow end of that range. The session in `server/src/providers/voice/openaiRealtime.ts` sets semantic_vad to `eagerness: 'auto'` with `create_response: false`. `server/src/calls/callSession.ts` then calls `respond()` only after semantic_vad reports `speech_stopped` and the speech gate confirms at least 160 ms of voiced audio; for "quiet" turns it waits for the transcript. Endpointing is the largest controllable contributor to the turn gap and can add about 500 ms before the model sees anything ([Softcery](https://www.softcery.com/lab/voice-agent-latency-budget-microphone-to-speaker)). In one Twilio example, semantic_vad at `auto` averaged about **1.26 s** of turn detection alone ([inworld-api-examples PR #112](https://github.com/inworld-ai/inworld-api-examples/pull/112)). On top of that come model time-to-first-audio and the PSTN leg. A Coval leaderboard, as reported by Cartesia (a competing vendor), puts GPT Realtime 2's median response at about **1,420 ms** ([Cartesia](https://www.cartesia.ai/learn/speech-to-speech-vs-cascaded)). CallBridge's server-side gate makes the agent safe against noise and "mm-hm" false triggers. The cost is that it stacks a second endpointer on top of the first.

The newest models remove that trade-off at the model level. GPT-Live-1, released to the API on **10 September 2026**, is a full-duplex "voice layer" that listens while speaking and delegates reasoning and tools to a backend text model. OpenAI reports a **~30-point Full-Duplex-Bench gain over gpt-realtime-2.1** ([DataNorth](https://datanorth.ai/news/openai-launches-gpt-live-1-in-the-api); [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-live-1-in-the-api/)). On Full-Duplex-Bench, which measures turn-taking and interruption, Grok Voice 3.0 scores 97.0%, GPT-Live-1 94.9% and Gemini 3.8 Live 91.9% ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/14/meet-grok-voice-3-0-spacexai-voice-agent-model-ranks-1-on-artificial-analysis/)). The same three models rank in the same order on the Speech Agent Arena human-preference Elo as presented by xAI: 1070, 1048 and 990 ([xAI](https://x.ai/news/grok-voice-3)). These two measures (duplex behavior and listener preference) track "sounds human on a call" far better than reasoning benchmarks like Big Bench Audio, which is now saturated near 99% ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-speech)).

Voice timbre matters less on the phone than it does in a web demo. The PSTN carries roughly 300–3,400 Hz in 8 kHz G.711, and anything above that is discarded ([Gradium](https://docs.gradium.ai/guides/recipes/telephony-audio.md)). TTS arena rankings are voted at full bandwidth, and the ~50 Elo gaps between top engines will shrink considerably over a phone line. Prosody, pacing and register survive the narrowband channel: short replies, varied acknowledgments, audible "let me check" moments instead of dead air. The Sesame demo that convinced many listeners it had crossed the uncanny valley deliberately included breaths, chuckles, hesitations and self-corrections ([ISPR/ZDNET](https://ispr.info/2025/03/04/masa-presence-sesame-ai-chatbot-is-crossing-the-uncanny-valley-of-conversational-voice/)). Some human-sounding agents also rely on cues whose purpose is to deceive. Retell and Vapi ship office and call-center background-sound presets ([Vapi support](https://support.vapi.ai/t/27563301/background-office-sounds)), and Dasha's docs state outright that ambient noise "reduces suspicion" of talking to an AI ([Dasha](https://blackbox.dasha.ai/docs/advanced-features/ambient-noise.md)). Part of the gap the user hears is therefore a gap CallBridge should keep: an agent that opens with "I'm an AI" will never be mistaken for a person, however good it sounds.

## Tuning the current stack can reclaim hundreds of milliseconds this month

CallBridge already does several things right. Audio is native `audio/pcmu` in both directions, so Twilio frames pass byte-for-byte with no resampling; that removes the aliasing and "metallic" artifacts a naive 24 kHz-to-8 kHz conversion would add. The speech gate already holds multilingual backchannels ("嗯", "はい", "oo/opo", "ajá"). Barge-in requires 400 ms of sustained speech within a 700 ms window, and truncation uses the actually-played position. Those choices match guidance from AssemblyAI, Twilio and Hamming: suppress barge-in for short or backchannel-only utterances, and treat backchannels, real interruptions and speech-like noise as separate problems ([AssemblyAI](https://assemblyai.com/docs/voice-agents/voice-agent-api/turn-detection-and-interruptions); [Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/insights/ai-voice-agent-interruption-handling); [Hamming](https://hamming.ai/blog/voice-agent-interruption-handling-runbook)). The remaining wins lie in turn latency and in what the model says.

**Measure first.** Instrument four intervals per turn: semantic_vad `speech_stopped` → gate decision; gate → `response.create`; `response.create` → first audio delta; first delta → first 20 ms frame sent to Twilio. Report p50 and p95, not averages ([Coval](https://www.coval.dev/blog/voice-ai-latency)). Without this breakdown, every later A/B result is uninterpretable. A reasonable target, measured at the bridge, is **p50 under 800 ms and p95 under ~1.2 s**; the callee hears an extra 100–300 ms of PSTN transit on top.

**Then turn the cheap dials.** Switch semantic_vad from `auto` to `medium`. In the Twilio example above, that change cut mean turn detection from ~1.26 s to ~1.10 s with no extra mid-sentence splits ([inworld-api-examples PR #112](https://github.com/inworld-ai/inworld-api-examples/pull/112)), and one builder found medium the only workable phone setting ([Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/i-built-a-voice-agent-on-openais-realtime-api-2da0977d4c51)). Pass it as a string, since a numeric value has been reported to break tool use ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/using-server-vad-breaks-tool-use-in-realtime-api/1362139)). A/B the `near_field` noise reduction currently enabled on input. One developer saw latency creep upward over long sessions with noise reduction plus semantic_vad, and it disappeared when they removed it ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/realtime-api-with-noise-reduction-has-sudden-increase-of-latency/1256390)). Keep reasoning effort at `low` or try `minimal` for conversational turns: higher effort adds latency, and the headline gpt-realtime-2 scores were measured at `xhigh` ([DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/gpt-realtime-2)). Host the Node bridge in US East, alongside Twilio's us1 media region and the OpenAI endpoint, because every relay hop is paid twice per turn ([Coval](https://www.coval.dev/blog/voice-ai-latency); [Twilio docs](https://www.twilio.com/docs/video/tutorials/video-regions-and-global-low-latency)).

**Reconsider how the gate spends time.** The architectural lesson from Quiq is that a classifier in front of the response adds its latency to every turn, while the same classifier used as a cancellation signal costs nothing ([Quiq](https://quiq.com/blog/why-the-interruption-problem-is-architecture/)). For "quiet" turns, CallBridge currently waits for the transcript before answering. One option is to issue `response.create` at `speech_stopped`, hold the first few hundred milliseconds of audio until the transcript arrives, and cancel if it turns out to be noise or a backchannel. This is an untested inference and should be measured against the false-reply rate it might introduce. Adding an acoustic end-of-turn model is another lever. LiveKit's vendor-run benchmark shows false-cutoff rates at a 300 ms budget ranging from 9.9% to 35.2% across models ([LiveKit](https://livekit.com/blog/solving-end-of-turn-detection)). Pipecat's Smart Turn v3 runs locally as ONNX across 23 languages ([Evalgent](https://www.evalgent.com/blog/pipecat-vs-livekit)), but whether it can run under onnxruntime-node, and whether it covers Tagalog, is unverified.

**Change the register, which costs nothing.** The biggest non-latency tell is a chatbot register: complete paragraphs, enumerations, recapping the question, identical confirmations. OpenAI's own Realtime prompting guide recommends a dedicated Pacing section (the speed parameter does not change speaking style), a variety rule (the model overuses sample phrases), a short clarification rule that is never repeated verbatim, and pinning the output language ([OpenAI cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/realtime_prompting_guide)). ElevenLabs' phone guidance adds: tell the model it is on a live phone call, keep replies to two or three sentences, and use brief affirmations such as "let me check that" ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-agents/best-practices/prompting-guide)). gpt-realtime-2 added **preambles**, short spoken acknowledgments while the model reasons or calls tools, plus audible recovery when a tool fails ([Agora](https://prod.agora.io/en/blog/gpt-realtime-2-is-here-and-preambles-change-how-voice-agents-feel)). With `create_response: false`, preambles only start once the gate fires, so they mask tool and reasoning time, not endpointing time. Relaying a question to the user is CallBridge's longest built-in silence: `QUESTION_NOTIFY_WAIT_MS` alone is 2.5 s, and the user then has to answer. The agent should immediately say a true, culturally appropriate hold phrase in the callee's language (for example "少々お待ちください" in Japanese, or "let me check with Ms. Li, one moment"), and never leave more than about a second of dead air.

**Choose voices by ear, per language.** The app currently restricts profiles to Marin and Cedar. No study was found rating either voice over G.711 in Mandarin, Japanese or Tagalog. A small blind test on real Twilio calls with native speakers is the only way to choose per language, and the voice cannot change mid-session.

Three observations follow from the table.

First, **GPT-Live-1 is the natural next step on OpenAI**, and it is not a parameter change. It uses a new endpoint (`v1/live/sessions`) and moves reasoning and tools into a backend model ([Coursiv](https://coursiv.io/blog/gpt-live-1-api)). That conflicts with CallBridge's design principle that the server, not the model, decides when the agent answers. A full-duplex model takes turns on its own. The policy layer would then have to work through the backend agent's tools and output gating instead of `create_response: false`. Whether GPT-Live-1 exposes any server-side turn control, μ-law I/O, or a usable Media Streams path (as opposed to only the SIP connector) are the first questions to answer from OpenAI's docs.

Second, **Eleven Realtime is the cheapest experiment**. Because it speaks the OpenAI Realtime protocol, the existing `OpenAIRealtimeAgent` might need little more than a different URL and voice ID. That is a claim to verify, especially for session fields such as `create_response`, `audio/pcmu` and `conversation.item.truncate`.

Third, **Grok Voice 3.0 leads on paper and is the only frontier speech-to-speech model with self-serve cloning**, but it is eight days old. Its telephony documentation is contested, its Illinois exclusion is a product constraint, and secondary sources disagree on even its corporate naming.

Benchmarks have real limits here. No published benchmark tests 8 kHz μ-law audio, or Mandarin, Japanese or Tagalog conversations. Listener preference and benchmark leaders often disagree ([Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/realtime-voice-models-benchmarks-vs-listener-preference)), and Coval measures vendor latencies well above what vendors claim ([Coval](https://www.coval.ai/blog/best-text-to-speech-providers-in-2026-how-to-choose-(and-why-vendor-benchmarks-lie)/)). For a Mandarin-first user base, Alibaba's `qwen3.8-omni-flash-realtime` (whose docs list Mandarin, Japanese and Tagalog) and StepFun's StepAudio 3 Realtime (99.7% on Big Bench Audio) deserve a listen ([Alibaba Cloud](https://www.alibabacloud.com/help/en/model-studio/s2s-model); [Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-speech)). No naturalness evidence was found for either, and both raise data-residency questions.

## Hybrid TTS pipelines buy voice choice and cloning at a latency cost

The alternative to a speech-to-speech model is a hybrid: keep gpt-realtime-2.1 for listening, reasoning, function calls and the speech gate, but switch its output to text and stream that text into a premium TTS engine that emits `ulaw_8000` directly. The OpenAI model page lists "Text, Audio" outputs, though no source confirmed configuration details for 2.1 ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime)). This is the only architecture that gives CallBridge a free choice of hundreds of voices, per-language voice routing, and broadly self-serve cloning while leaving its policy layer intact. A full cascade (streaming STT → text LLM → TTS) also qualifies, but discards the Realtime session CallBridge has already engineered around.

The TTS frontier rotates almost weekly. Through August–October 2026 the top of Artificial Analysis's Speech Arena moved among Cartesia Sonic 3.6, ElevenLabs Eleven v4 and v4 Turbo, Inworld Realtime TTS-2 and HeyGen Voice, which usually sit within 50–100 Elo of each other ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/08/18/cartesia-ships-sonic-3-6-a-streaming-tts-model-that-now-leads-both-artificial-analysis-speech-arenas/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/28/elevenlabs-new-v4-speech-model-supports-more-expression-control-and-90-languages/); [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/heygen-s-voice-turbo-tops-speech-leaderboard-at-87-less-cost)). HeyGen's measured ~2 s round trip rules it out for live calls ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/heygen/heygen-voice-1-turbo)). The streaming-capable short-list is therefore Sonic 3.6, Eleven v4 Turbo and Inworld TTS-2. **Language fit decides between them more than overall rank does.** On Artificial Analysis's September 2026 multilingual arenas, voted by first-language speakers, **Sonic 3.6 led 7 of 9 languages including Japanese**, while **Inworld TTS-2 led Mandarin** (1,185 Elo vs Sonic 3.6 at 1,146) ([Artificial Analysis](https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2102490340678856997); [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/cartesia-s-sonic-3-6-dominates-eight-of-nine-global-voice-ai-leaderboards)). Those arenas predate Eleven v4. **No Tagalog benchmark exists anywhere.** Only ElevenLabs (Flash v2.5, v3) and Azure (fil-PH DragonHD, preview) clearly list Filipino ([Replicate](https://replicate.com/elevenlabs/flash-v2.5); [Azure](https://docs.azure.cn/en-us/ai-services/speech-service/high-definition-voices)). Hume Octave 2, Rime and Deepgram Aura-2 lack Mandarin and Tagalog, which rules them out ([Hume](https://hume.ai/blog/octave-2-launch)).

The trade-offs are concrete. A hybrid adds one TTS time-to-first-audio (roughly 75–260 ms) plus a short text buffer before speech begins. It also loses the speech-to-speech model's coupling of meaning and prosody, because the TTS sees only text. Barge-in then has to cancel both the Realtime response and the TTS stream before clearing Twilio. Benchmarks disagree on the net latency effect. One production benchmark of 10+ projects measured speech-to-speech p50 at 540–580 ms against roughly 700 ms for cascades ([Destilabs](https://www.destilabs.com/blog/ai-voice-agent-benchmark-2026)), while a from-scratch cascade study measured a 958 ms median ([Gradium](https://gradium.ai/content/cascaded-voice-agent-vs-speech-to-speech-2026)). Cost likely does not rise. Audio-output tokens are the expensive part of Realtime, so switching to text output should offset TTS fees; the notes estimate **$0.07–$0.12/min all-in** for the hybrid, against roughly **$0.10–$0.20/min** for today's all-audio stack. Both figures are inferences from token prices and need measurement.

Managed platforms were also considered, and they mostly fail CallBridge's control requirements. Twilio ConversationRelay ($0.07/min plus voice and LLM; Twilio-reported 491 ms p50 / 713 ms p95) is the lowest-effort way to get an ElevenLabs voice, including custom voice IDs, on the existing Twilio account ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/twiml/connect/conversationrelay); [Thunderphone](https://thunderphone.com/guides/twilio-conversationrelay-pricing)). But it is text-in/text-out, so Twilio would take over speech-to-text, turn-taking and barge-in, and whether listen-in can run alongside it is unconfirmed. Other platforms would replace the Media Streams bridge, the speech gate and listen-in: Retell's custom-LLM WebSocket, Vapi's live call control, and ElevenLabs Agents' "register call" mode that keeps Twilio under your control. All-in prices for these cluster at $0.07–$0.33/min ([Retell docs](https://docs.retellai.com/api-references/llm-websocket); [Vapi docs](https://docs.vapi.ai/calls/call-features); [ElevenLabs docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/agents-platform/phone-numbers/twilio-integration/register-call)). Their most useful role is as a reference: call a Retell or ElevenLabs demo agent to hear the target CallBridge is aiming for. LiveKit Agents is the only mainstream framework with a Node SDK and native SIP, but adopting it means rebuilding the call leg ([Evalgent](https://www.evalgent.com/blog/pipecat-vs-livekit)).

## Cloning the user's voice works, but adds the least value and the most risk

Cloning is now commodity technology. Self-serve instant cloning from roughly **3–30 seconds** of clean audio is offered by ElevenLabs IVC (Starter plan, ~$6/mo), Cartesia (3–10 s), Inworld (3 s minimum, cloning free), MiniMax, Fish Audio, Hume, and Google Chirp 3 Instant Custom Voice (≤10 s plus a separate consent recording) ([Magichour](https://magichour.ai/blog/elevenlabs-pricing); [Cartesia](https://cartesia.ai/product/voice-cloning); [Inworld](https://inworld.ai/resources/best-voice-cloning-api); [Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/text-to-speech/docs/chirp3-instant-custom-voice)). Professional fine-tuned clones need far more audio. One review cites 3–6 hours for ElevenLabs PVC ([Coval](https://www.coval.ai/blog/elevenlabs-review-2026-voice-cloning-and-synthesis-capabilities-explained/)), and PVC trains against `eleven_multilingual_v2` ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/voices/professional-voice-cloning)), which may be slower than the Flash and Turbo models used for live calls. That needs checking. Vendors quote the same latency for instant clones as for stock voices, though no independent measurement was found.

The paths that fit CallBridge are narrower. OpenAI's recorded custom voices work across Realtime but are "limited to eligible customers" through sales ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/custom-voices)). Azure Personal Voice is limited-access for approved use cases ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/ai-services/speech-service/personal-voice-overview)). Eleven Realtime's support for cloned voices is unconfirmed. In practice, that leaves **the hybrid TTS architecture or Grok Voice 3.0** as the way to give CallBridge the user's voice in Phase 3.

Four product realities temper the appeal.

**Accent leakage.** A Mandarin speaker's clone speaking English, Japanese or Tagalog will carry the source language's phonetics, and this is the best-documented failure mode of cross-lingual cloning ([KIT IWSLT 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07240v1)). Raising speaker similarity tends to raise accent copying, since "higher SIM can also reflect stronger source-accent copying" ([arXiv 2610.07524](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.07524)). A Google patent even found that raters judged heavily accented synthetic speech *more* similar to the target speaker ([USPTO 11580952](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11580952)). CallBridge exists because the user's accented speech is hard for callees to follow, so a faithful clone partly reintroduces the problem. Cartesia's Localize feature and accent-intensity controls address this, and a per-user "accent strength" setting is worth prototyping ([Cartesia](https://www.cartesia.ai/sonic)).

**Narrowband audio.** Everything above roughly 3.4–4 kHz, including much of what makes a timbre distinctive (sibilance, breathiness), is discarded on the phone. The "sounds like me" benefit therefore shrinks over the line. No published test of clone similarity after G.711 compression was found.

**The third-person paradox.** CallBridge's disclosure rule means the agent must speak *about* the user ("calling on behalf of Ms. Li"), never *as* the user. A clone of Ms. Li's voice saying "I'm calling for Ms. Li" is odd for strangers. Its real value lies in calls to friends and family, who are exactly the listeners most likely to believe they are hearing her live.

**Tagalog.** Only ElevenLabs clearly advertises Filipino cloning ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/languages/filipino)), so a stock-voice fallback will be needed.

Consent practice across the industry varies widely. Consumer Reports found in March 2025 that four of six cloning services, ElevenLabs among them, relied on a checkbox self-attestation, and singled out Descript's recorded consent statement as the strongest ([Consumer Reports](https://innovation.consumerreports.org/new-report-do-these-6-ai-voice-cloning-companies-do-enough-to-prevent-misuse/)). ElevenLabs PVC requires the owner to read a captcha phrase aloud ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/voices/professional-voice-cloning)). Google requires a fixed-script consent recording in the target language ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/text-to-speech/docs/chirp3-instant-custom-voice)). Cartesia states that "all voice clones require verified consent from the speaker" ([Cartesia](https://www.cartesia.ai/product/voice-cloning)). No consumer product was found that places outbound calls in the user's cloned voice. CallBridge would be early here, which is a reason to over-build safeguards rather than under-build them.

## Disclosure stays fixed, and calls to cell phones carry the real legal exposure

Federal law treats every option in this report the same way. The FCC's **February 2024 Declaratory Ruling** holds that AI-generated voices, "including voice cloning", are "artificial" voices under the TCPA ([FCC](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-makes-ai-generated-voices-robocalls-illegal); [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/fcc-requires-consent-for-ai-generated-cloned-voice-calls/)). Calls using them therefore need the called party's **prior express consent** unless an exemption applies. They must also identify the entity responsible for the call at the start ([Steptoe](https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/steptechtoe-blog/fcc-bans-robocalls-using-ai-generated-voices.html)), and Twilio repeats both duties to its customers ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/fcc-ruling-ai-generated-voices-robocalls)). Penalties run **$500–$1,500 per call** ([Brownstein](https://www.bhfs.com/insight/fcc-declares-ai-generated-calls-subject-to-tcpa/)).

How this applies depends on who is being called. For **businesses**, the landline consent rule covers *residential* lines, so CallBridge's main use case (calling a clinic or restaurant landline) is likely outside it; this reading comes from the statute's structure, not a retrieved source. For **cell phones**, the TCPA's rule has no non-commercial carve-out, which makes "relay a message to my friend's mobile" CallBridge's **highest-risk use case regardless of voice**; a cloned voice does not change that analysis. It is also unsettled whether CallBridge or the user counts as the call's initiator when the app dials at the user's direction. Since *McLaughlin v. McKesson* (June 2025), district courts are not bound by FCC interpretations ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-07-01-mclaughlin-chiropractic-us-supreme-court-invites-new-era-of-tcpa-jurisprudence)), but betting that a court would decide a live LLM voice is not "artificial" would be unwise. The FCC's 2024 proposal (FCC 24-84) to require AI disclosure at the start of every call **remains unadopted** as of October 2026, according to vendor sources ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-09-10/html/2024-19028.htm); [econsent.org](https://econsent.org/posts/ai-voice-agents-tcpa-rules-october-2026/)), so CallBridge's existing disclosure already exceeds current federal law.

State law adds edges rather than walls. **California AB 2905** requires an "artificial voice" disclosure, but its text targets prerecorded autodialer messages, so a live conversational agent "sits at the edge of the text rather than inside it" ([Delatorre Review](https://www.thedelatorrereview.com/californias-ai-voice-disclosure-rule-when-robocalls-must-tell-you-the-voice-is-artificial/)). **Utah** requires disclosure when a consumer asks ([AI Laws by State](https://www.ailawsbystate.com/disclosure-guide)), and **Maine** reportedly requires notice wherever a reasonable consumer could be misled ([Conferbot](https://www.conferbot.com/blog/ai-chatbot-disclosure-laws-compliance-guide), single vendor source). **Wiretap and transcription law is a larger, voice-independent risk.** *Ambriz v. Google* let CIPA claims proceed because a transcription vendor *could* use call data for its own purposes ([ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/privacy/federal-judge-allows-google-customer-service-ai-class-action-to-proceed/)). CallBridge should say "this call is transcribed" in its opening line and contractually bar every speech vendor from training on call audio, which matters more as a bake-off adds vendors. Voice-likeness laws such as Tennessee's ELVIS Act and Illinois' digital-replica law protect against *unauthorized* use, so they do not bar self-cloning ([Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2024/04/tennessee-elvis-act-ai-voice-replica)). Illinois BIPA covers voiceprints, however, so an Illinois user's voice model may need written consent, a retention policy and a destruction schedule ([Promise Legal](https://blog.promise.legal/ai-voice-cloning-musician-rights/)).

Provider contracts make disclosure mandatory even where statutes do not. OpenAI's service terms require customers to "clearly disclose that generated speech is AI-generated" and bar reproducing anyone's voice without express consent ([OpenAI Service Terms](https://openai.com/policies/service-terms/)). Twilio's AI addendum forbids using its features "to mislead anyone that an Output was solely human generated" ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/legal/ai-terms/predictive-generative-ai-features)). ElevenLabs bans cloning without "explicit and informed consent" and bans stripping watermarks or provenance signals ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/use-policy)). The fraud surface matters too: a Wall Street Journal reporter beat her own bank's voice authentication with a clone of herself ([BankInfoSecurity](https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/ai-deepfakes-push-banks-beyond-voice-authentication-a-32388)), and the FTC's proposed extension of its impersonation rule would reach tool providers that know or should know their tools are used to impersonate ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/02/ftc-proposes-new-protections-combat-ai-impersonation-individuals)).

The ethical line was drawn in 2018. Google Duplex called a salon using "hmm" and "uh" without disclosing it was a bot, and the backlash forced Google to promise "disclosure built-in" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2018/05/11/google-duplex-virtual-assistant-ethical-issues-ai-machine-learning); [Loop Insight](https://loopinsight.com/2018/05/11/google-now-says-controversial-ai-voice-calling-system-will-identify-itself-to-humans)). People cannot reliably detect synthetic speech ([arXiv 2301.07829](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.07829)), so a voice as human as the ones the user heard, without disclosure, would deceive in practice.

For CallBridge this means natural prosody, fast turn-taking, real acknowledgments ("got it") and *true* hold phrases are allowed. **Synthetic office ambience, fake typing, scripted disfluencies whose only purpose is to seem human, and first-person impersonation of the user are off-limits**, and "are you a robot?" always gets an immediate "yes, I'm an AI". A workable opening line is: "Hi, this is an AI assistant calling for [Name] through CallBridge. [Name] doesn't speak English fluently, so I'm helping. This call is transcribed." With a cloned voice it becomes: "…an AI assistant using a synthetic copy of [Name]'s voice." Re-disclose after holds and transfers. Separately, A-level STIR/SHAKEN attestation through Twilio Trust Hub and a branded caller ID will do more for answer rates than any voice upgrade ([Twilio](https://twilio.com/blog/shaken-stir-sign-twilio-calls)).

## Recommendation: tune now, bake off next, clone last

The evidence supports a clear order of work. Turn latency and register are the dominant causes of the gap and are fixable inside the current code. Full-duplex models are the likely step change, but they reshape CallBridge's control model, so they must earn their place in a blind test. Cloning is the least valuable and most hazardous lever, so it belongs at the end, gated on evidence that callees and users actually benefit.

| Phase | Timing | Work | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Quick wins in the current stack** | Weeks 1–2 | Per-turn latency instrumentation (four intervals, p50/p95). Semantic_vad `auto` → `medium`. A/B `near_field` noise reduction on/off. Reasoning effort `low` vs `minimal`. Co-locate the bridge in US East. Phone-register prompt rewrite: pacing section, 1–2 sentence turns, variety rule, spoken numbers, language pin, preamble rules. Immediate in-language hold phrase when relaying to the user. Trial speculative response for "quiet" turns. Blind Marin-vs-Cedar test per language on real calls. Opening line adds "this call is transcribed". | p50 turn gap under 800 ms, p95 under 1.2 s at the bridge, with no rise in false replies or false barge-ins |
| **2. A/B bake-off behind `VoiceAgent`** | Weeks 3–6 | Adapters for: (a) tuned gpt-realtime-2.1 baseline; (b) Eleven Realtime v1 Mini/Max via the OpenAI protocol; (c) GPT-Live-1 (first verify μ-law support, server-side turn control and Twilio path); (d) Grok Voice 3.0 (verify SIP or WebSocket bridge); (e) hybrid gpt-realtime-2.1 text-out → Sonic 3.6 / Eleven v4 Turbo / Inworld TTS-2 with `ulaw_8000`, routed per language. Optional cost-floor arm: Gemini 3.8 Live. Scripted calls in EN/ZH/JA/ES/TL to consenting test callees over real PSTN, recorded, then rated blind by native speakers for naturalness and comprehensibility. Also measure turn gap, false cutoff and barge-in rates, task success, policy compliance (disclosure delivered, never claims human, answers "are you AI?" truthfully) and cost per minute. Confirm vendor no-training terms before any real-user traffic. | One architecture wins on blind ratings in at least the EN, ZH and JA calls without losing policy-layer authority; Tagalog fallback chosen |
| **3. Opt-in self-cloning with safeguards** | After Phase 2, only if the winner supports it (hybrid TTS or Grok) | Onboarding: ~60 s read script plus a live, randomized spoken consent phrase whose speaker embedding must match the sample; consent audio stored. Clone bound to the verified account holder, usable only on that user's calls, never exportable, deletable in one tap. Illinois BIPA consent and retention schedule (and no Grok-based clones there). Voice-specific disclosure ("a synthetic copy of [Name]'s voice"); third-person speech only. Block or warn on banks, brokerages, carriers and government lines. Accent-strength setting. Stock-voice fallback for unsupported languages. Keep provider watermarks intact. Pilot first on calls to friends and family who have agreed to AI calls, which also addresses the TCPA cell-phone consent question. | Blind tests show the clone improves user satisfaction without lowering callee comprehension, and counsel signs off |

A legal review should run alongside Phase 2 rather than after it, covering who initiates CallBridge calls for TCPA purposes, what consent flow covers calls to individuals' mobile numbers, whether a callback number belongs in the opening, and all-party-consent transcription notices. The answers do not depend on which voice wins, so waiting for the bake-off only delays launch.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the user's question. "Sounding human" on a phone line is mostly a matter of when the agent speaks, how briefly, and how gracefully it yields. Narrowband audio erases much of what separates a good voice from a great one. That is good news for CallBridge: the levers that matter most are the ones it owns. The speech gate built to stop the agent answering "mm-hm" is also the main source of its hesitant rhythm, and it can be made faster without being made dumber. The deeper strategic tension is that the most human-sounding models now achieve that by taking over turn-taking themselves, which runs directly against CallBridge's principle that the server decides. The bake-off has to price that trade explicitly, not just rank voices.

Cloning turns out to be a feature whose value and risk sit in the same place. A clone of the user's voice matters to people who know that voice, and those are the people most likely to forget they are talking to software. Treating disclosure as a design asset rather than a handicap is the defensible position, legally under the TCPA and provider terms, and as product positioning: an assistant that says plainly "I'm an AI helping Ms. Li, who doesn't speak English well" will earn more patience than one that tries to pass. CallBridge can match rival agents on rhythm and register without copying their disguises.
